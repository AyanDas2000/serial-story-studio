# Episode 5: The Wheel and the Switch

Draft, not approved.

The technician came up the service stairs a little after noon with Maya a step behind him. He was a thin man in a company jacket with a clipboard of his own, and he stopped inside the tape and took off his cap. Moss watched him from the gravel and did not look away.

"Terrible thing," the technician said. "They told me at the office. Poor man, lying right there in the middle aisle."

Ron was standing by the tank room door with Arjun and the owner. He did not turn his head. "Who told you the middle aisle?"

"Somebody did. The office, maybe."

"Sergeant," Ron said, "did you tell him where?"

"I told him a man was found dead," Maya said. "Nothing else."

The technician shrugged. It was a small shrug, and it came a moment late.

Ron took the clipboard from its nail inside the plywood door and held it open. "This is your last fill, two days back. Look at the quantity of concentrate, and then at the lines above it. Eleven weeks of entries, and none of them within a third of this one. You mixed this drum much too strong, in your own hand."

"The season changes. Pests."

"The season didn't change it on that line only." Ron closed the clipboard. "I'll tell you what I think, and you can correct me where I'm wrong. You filled the drum strong. The timer didn't need touching, because the timer was never going to be what set it off. You came back when no pin was due, threw the manual switch under that cracked cover, and left. Mist ran in a sealed room. The plants drank it. A watchman who bolted the door the way he'd been told to had nowhere to go."

"You can't know that."

"The student can tell me the wheel stood well short of both pegs and that only two were ever set. He checks it every week so the trays won't be soaked, and he checked it the Tuesday before. The mist wasn't due. Somebody ran it." Ron looked at the gray lever. "I didn't see that at first. I wanted a habit to be the killer, because it needed nobody. It was a good picture, and it was wrong."

"Anyone could reach that switch."

"Anyone with a key to this door," said the owner, hoarsely. "I gave three. Him, me, the building."

The technician looked at Arjun, as if a lawyer might help him, and Arjun looked back with no expression at all. Then the technician looked at the glass, and the dead ferns, and the stairwell door, and Maya moved a half step to stand in it.

"I mixed it by the book," he said.

"Then the sheet will say so," Maya said, "and the drum will say so, and you can explain it to someone with a recorder." She took out the cuffs without hurry. "I'm arresting you on suspicion of causing the death of the night watchman. The rest we'll do at the station."

He didn't fight it. He stood with his wrists out and his cap still in one hand, and when she walked him toward the stairs he glanced once down the aisle, at the far bench. At the orchid.

Ron saw it. "Why that one?" he called after him.

The technician said nothing. The stairwell took him down.

For a while the room was only wind on glass. Maya came back up alone, wrote in her notebook against the doorframe, and said, "He'll talk or he won't. The drum goes to the lab, and the log goes in a bag. You've got your shape, Ron."

"I've got most of it. Not the orchid."

"You'll have it when he does."

Arjun drew a folder from under his arm and held it out. "The lease, the service contract, the building's agreements. My client wants you to have all of it, since you'll want to know who profits from an empty greenhouse."

"Thank you."

Maya went down to see about the drum. The owner sat on an upturned crate, looking at the benches. Ron took the folder to the potting bench and turned the pages, one signature block at a time, out of habit more than hope. He passed the service contract. He passed a rider on the tank installation.

On the last page of the lease, in the line for the building's previous holder, there was a name.

He read it once. Then he did not read it again, because he did not need to. He knew the hand, and the surname that went with the hand, and he knew a kitchen table where he had last seen it in ink.

"Anything?" Arjun asked from the door.

"Nothing that matters today." Ron squared the pages and closed the folder, and his voice was perfectly level. "I'll read the rest tonight."

On the gravel outside, Moss stood up and came to the door, and stayed pressed against Ron's leg.
