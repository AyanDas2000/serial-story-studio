# Episode 3: The Theory That Fit

Draft, not approved.

Ron found the tank behind a plywood door at the east end, between the potting bench and the wall. It was a gray plastic drum on a stand, with a lid, a hose manifold, and a timer box bolted beside it. From the manifold, thin lines climbed to the ceiling and ran the length of the room, ending in small misting nozzles above each bench.

"The misters," Ron said. "When do they run?"

The owner stood in the doorway with Arjun at his shoulder. "Before dawn, mostly. The service sets the timer and mixes the feed. Fertilizer, something for pests, whatever the season wants."

"Mixed in this drum."

"Yes."

Ron lifted the lid with two fingers. A sour, mineral smell rose out of it, and he held his breath and put the lid down. Inside, the drum was nearly empty, with a gray crust dried along the waterline. He crouched and looked at the nozzle nearest him. The same gray bloom had crystallized around the tip, thicker than anywhere else in the room.

"Look at the film," he said. "It's on the benches, the pots, the iron on the door, and it's heaviest at the nozzles. Something went through those lines and came out as a fog. Everything in the room breathed it, and everything in the room was wet with it. The plants died because they were drinking it." He straightened. "And a man on his rounds came into a closed room full of it."

Maya had her notebook out. "A fog doesn't kill a man on the spot."

"It does if it's strong enough and the room is sealed. Glass, rubber weatherstrip, putty in every pane. We spent an hour proving how tight it is." He turned to the door. "And the bolt answers itself. Does your watchman bolt the door when he's up here?"

The owner nodded slowly. "The latch doesn't hold in a wind. They all bolt it from inside. I told them to."

"Then nobody locked him in. He did what he always did, and he was inside with the mist when it ran." Ron let the words settle. "No locked room, no mystery, only a habit that killed him."

Arjun let out a breath. Maya wrote for a while without looking up. "That fits the stairwell, the pry bar, the maintenance man," she said. "It fits no wound. If the examiner finds it in his lungs, it fits everything."

"Then someone put it in the drum," the owner said. His voice was thick. "Somebody mixed it wrong, or on purpose."

"The service mixes it." Ron pointed. On a nail inside the plywood door hung a clipboard with a log sheet, its last line written in a fast, looping scrawl and dated two days back. "That's the last fill. Whoever signed it handled this drum last, and knew the timer."

"The technician," Arjun said.

"I'd start there. Sergeant, you'll want him found and asked what went into this tank."

Maya was already speaking into her radio. Ron stood back and felt the room agree with him, the way a lock gives when the key is right. The owner leaned against the doorframe, relieved to have a shape for his grief. Arjun was nodding. Even the gray film seemed to have been arranged to say it.

He looked down the length of the room and his eye went, as it had all morning, to the far bench.

"One problem," he said, mostly to himself.

"The orchid," said Maya, without turning.

"Same lines run over that bench. Same nozzle." He walked toward it. Above the pot hung a mister crusted gray like the others. "If the fog killed everything, it should have killed that. And if someone carried it in afterward, I still don't know who, or why they'd bother."

"Maybe it's tougher than the rest," Arjun offered.

"Maybe." Ron said it politely. He crouched again at the clean ring on the wood. Behind him the radio crackled and Maya gave an address to someone, and the technician's scrawl became a name on a list.

The owner had followed him down the aisle. He stopped a few feet away and stood looking at the pale flowers.

"Mr. Calder," he said. "You asked who last brought a plant up here."

"I did."

"Nobody did. Not in the thirty years. I don't keep orchids." He swallowed. "I never owned that one."

Ron did not move. Down the aisle, Maya had stopped speaking into her radio.
