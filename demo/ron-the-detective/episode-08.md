# Episode 8: The Gate in the Lane

Draft, not approved.

Maya's closet was narrow and smelled of cedar. The shoebox sat on the top shelf behind a winter hat. She set it on the bed under the lamp and lifted the lid, and Ron stayed standing by the door with Moss against his leg. He had said he would not tell the story whole again, so he said nothing.

The notebook was a cheap one with a green cover gone soft at the corners. Maya turned pages with one finger. "Here. That's me, the junior with the clipboard." Her handwriting was upright and careful. It listed times the corridor doors opened and closed, and the names of officers coming and going. Then it listed a canvass of the rear lane, house by house, with a tick or a dash beside each.

"Read it to me," Ron said. "Exactly as written, not as you remember it."

She read it. Most lines ended in a dash: no answer, nobody in. Near the bottom she stopped. "Bakery at the lane end. Lights on from four. Not reached, shift ended." She looked up. "Nobody went back. I handed the sheet in and that was the end of it."

"So no one has ever asked him."

"No one asked anyone on that page. We had your statement by morning."

That landed, and Ron let it. "Is it still there?"

"I walk past it on the way to the market. The lights are always on early."

They did not sleep. At a quarter to four they stood in the lane behind the old house, which was now a dentist's with a plastic sign. The air smelled of yeast. Halfway down, a rectangle of yellow light fell across the cobbles, and a man in a floury apron was hauling a rack of loaves onto a handcart.

He was past sixty, wide through the arms. He looked at Maya's badge, then at the dog, then went on stacking loaves. "I'm busy till six," he said. "You can talk to my back."

"That's fine," Maya said. "It's about a night some years ago, in the house on this lane that's a dentist now. A man was arrested there."

"I remember the police. Cars at the front till noon." He slid a tray in. "Nobody came round the back. I'd have said."

"What would you have said?" Ron asked.

The baker glanced at him, then back at the cart. "That I do the same round every morning, and have done thirty years. I come out of here at ten to four with the bags and I walk the lane to the far end. Every house has a nail or a hook on the gatepost, and I hang the bread on it. That one had a nail on the outside of the gate." He tapped the side of the cart. "I never found that gate open. Not once. There was a chain through the bars, wrapped twice, with a padlock hanging on the yard side. You couldn't reach the lock from the lane. I know because I looked, the first week, when they hadn't told me where to hang the bag."

Ron kept very still. "Never open. Not even once."

"Not that I saw, and I saw it every morning at ten to four. The morning after, same." The baker shrugged. "They had the chain through even with the police in the front. I remember thinking that was tidy of someone."

Maya was writing. Her pen had stopped halfway through a word.

"A man coming in from the lane," Ron said carefully, "couldn't have come through that gate. Not unless somebody on the yard side let him in."

"Or he came over the wall." The baker nodded down the lane. "That wall was higher than a man. Broken glass in the top, at the time. Ask whoever lived there."

"I did," Ron said, and the baker looked at him oddly, because it had sounded like a confession.

They thanked him, and he did not seem to think he had given them anything. They walked up the lane in the gray before dawn. The wall was still high, with a line of old mortar along the top where something had been cemented and then ground away. Moss stopped at the gate, which was a modern one now, and sniffed the bottom bar.

"I put him in the yard," Ron said. "I told you that at the table, and I told it to the old man. A man crossing from the lane at some hour in the dark. I saw it. I saw the whole crossing, Maya, the gravel and the turn by the shed. I never saw the gate."

"You didn't ask?"

"It never came up. I gave the crossing to him and he signed it." He put a palm flat on the wall, feeling the rough place where the glass had been. "Nothing in my reconstruction said how a man got in. I filled the yard with him and never gave him a way through."

Maya turned back to the notebook, paging by the thin light of the streetlamp. She stopped three pages from the canvass, where a line sat alone with a ring around it. She read it twice before she read it aloud.

"'Mr. said gate was chained that night. Statement later: not mentioned.'" She looked up. "It's his own words, Ron. He told me in the corridor, on the way out, and I wrote it down. Then it wasn't in the statement."

"Because I never put it there."

"Because the story didn't need it."

Light was coming up behind the dentist's roof. Ron took his hand off the wall. He thought of a woman in a gray cardigan buttoned wrong, who had told him he could ask about the building.

"She said to ask her about the building," he said. "I'm going to ask her about a gate."
