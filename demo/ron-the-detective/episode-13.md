# Episode 13: The Needle's Bearing

Draft, not approved.

Ron did not wait for the others to leave before he spread the street map over the cloth, with the two compasses weighing down its corners.

"Listen to this one," he said, "and then tell me where it breaks. I'll mark which parts are seen and which are imagined."

Maya folded her arms. Arjun had taken the woman to the door, and she stopped there with her coat half on.

"Seen," Ron said. "Two compasses, a few days from true, both leaning the same way, by an angle I can't separate with a lens. Seen: a mark under the polished patch on one, and the same mark open on the other. Seen: someone rubbed at that mark for years. Imagined, all of it, from here." He tapped the brass. "A man doesn't keep a faulty instrument beside his bed. He keeps a corrected one. The error isn't the fault. It's the message. Someone set this needle off true so that a person who knew would read a bearing off it, and whoever carries it learns where to walk."

"That's a great deal to hang on an angle," Arjun said from the door.

"It is. That's why I'm going to test it with a pencil."

He set the widow's compass on the map at the one place he was certain of, the yard behind the old house, where it had lain by the shed. He read the needle's lean against a protractor from Maya's bag and ruled a line from that point at the offset angle. It ran across the yard, over the high wall, across the lane, and down the back of the block. Beyond the lane it crossed the bakery's rear.

"Not the bakery," Maya said.

"Before the bakery. The wall." He put his finger on the first spot the line crossed. "The baker told us the top was cemented with glass and later ground smooth. That's a place where someone worked on masonry. If you wanted to hide something outside a house with a chained gate, where nobody could reach it from the lane, you'd put it in the wall itself."

"You're building a story again."

"I'm building a hole, and I'll know in an hour whether it's there." He looked up. "That's different. Come with me."

The dentist's receptionist let them into the yard because Maya showed her badge and Ron kept his voice small. The shed was gone. A rack of bicycles stood where it had been. Moss sat at the bottom step and watched Ron pace from the rack to the wall with the compass held flat, turning until the needle's lean pointed him at the brickwork.

It brought him to a stretch of wall three bricks along from a drainpipe. The old mortar line ran across the top, ground to a pale scar.

"Here," he said.

Maya borrowed a stepladder from the receptionist and climbed it. She ran a gloved hand along the coping, pressed each brick, and then crouched with a torch, looking at the joints. Ron stood below with his heart going harder than he liked.

"It's solid," she said after a long time. "The mortar is old and unbroken. Nothing has been taken out of this wall since whoever ground off the glass. No gaps, no loose coping. Ron, there's a dead spider in the crack. It's been dead a while."

"Check the course below."

"I've checked three courses." She came down, brushing her knees. "It's a wall."

He stood with the compass in his palm. The needle leaned a few degrees off true, as it always did, patient and sure. He felt the particular flatness he had known twice before, in a corridor and then in a kitchen, when a thing he had seen whole turned out to have a single missing joint.

"I put the line through the wall because the wall had been worked on," he said. "That's the only reason. Nothing in the needle said wall."

"You also started from the yard."

"Because the widow's compass was found in the yard."

"Found," Maya said. "Not kept. Ron, the woman's father had the other one on his bedside table for years. You said the error is a bearing. A bearing from where?"

He looked at her. Then he looked at the compass in his hand, the one that had lain in the dirt of a yard for a night and then in a tin for twenty years, and at its twin, bright where a thumb had worn it, which had lived beside a bed.

"I set the line from a place the thing was dropped," he said slowly. "Not from where anyone sat and read it."

"So?"

"So the needle may not mean anything out here at all." He pulled out his phone and found the number the woman had left on Arjun's receipt. "It would mean something from the room where he kept it. It might mean something from the bed."

She was still on the step when the call connected, her coat in her hand. Ron could see her through Maya's shoulder, standing in his doorway across the city of his own mind, waiting for him to say what he wanted.

"Is your father's flat still as he left it?" Ron asked. "I need to stand where that table stood."
