# Episode 6: The Name on the Lease

Draft, not approved.

The lease gave a mailing address for notices, typed small beneath the signatures, and Ron copied it onto the back of his hand with Maya's pen because his notebook was in the car. The address was a third-floor flat above a shuttered shoe repairer, ten minutes from the greenhouse. He went at dusk and took Moss, who climbed the stairs one step behind him.

The woman who opened the door was perhaps sixty, small, in a gray cardigan buttoned wrong by one button. She looked at Ron, then down at the dog, then at Ron again. She did not seem surprised. She stood back from the door and said, "You'd better come in, then."

The flat was one room and a kitchen. A narrow bed was made tight as a drum. A kettle sat on a single ring, and a radio murmured low on the sill. There were two chairs at the table, though she lived alone, and one of them had a folded newspaper laid on the seat as if to keep it from being used. Moss lay down by the door without being asked.

"I'm sorry to come unannounced," Ron said. "I'm working on a matter at the building where the greenhouse is. Your family's name is on the old lease as the previous holder."

"It would be." She filled the kettle. "Nobody has come about that building in years."

"Whose hand signed it?"

She turned the ring on before she answered. "He's not here to ask, Mr. Calder."

He had not given his name. He said nothing about that. She set out two cups and kept her back to him a moment, and Ron looked at the table. It was square, scarred pale at one corner, and the light fell across it from a window with a nail-hole in the frame. He had sat at a table like it once, with a pen laid on a sheet between himself and a man who did not want to pick it up.

His stomach tightened, slowly, the way a hand closes.

"You remember me," he said.

"I remember a young man who was very sure." She poured. "He explained to my husband what had happened, every step of it, as if he had watched. He was kind about it. That was the worst of it." She set a cup in front of him and sat in the chair without the newspaper. "Some of it was true. I'll say that, because it's fair. But he didn't know the rest, and he said it all the same."

Ron held the cup without drinking. "Is he living?"

"Not for a long while." She looked at the window. "Drink your tea. I'm not going to say any more tonight. If you want the building, ask me about the building. If you want the other, you can come back when you've decided which."

She said nothing further about either. He finished the tea out of manners, thanked her, and she walked him to the door without a word. On the stairs Moss pressed against his knee the whole way down.

Ron called them to his own kitchen at nine, and they came: Maya in her work jacket, Arjun with the folder, which he set on the table and did not open. Moss took his place under Ron's chair.

"I read the lease," Ron said. "The last page."

"I wondered," said Arjun, "why you went quiet."

"There's a name on it. I'd like to tell you both why it matters, and I've never told you both at once, because each of you got a different half." He sat. "Maya, you know I left the force and that there was a case. Arjun, you know I had a hearing, and that I wouldn't discuss it."

"I know what the file says," Maya said. "Not why."

"The file says I took a statement the man later withdrew. The file doesn't say I wrote most of it. I sat at his kitchen table and laid out how it must have gone. Each piece fit, and it felt like seeing. I told it to him so completely and so kindly that he signed what I'd told him." Ron's voice stayed flat. "A good deal of it was true. A detail wasn't. A man who wasn't there that night, or I think wasn't, was put there by me. When the detail failed, the rest failed with it. The case fell apart and so did he. I left before they asked me to."

Arjun had gone very still. "And the name on the lease?"

"His. His hand. His wife is alive, and she lives eight minutes from that greenhouse in a flat where I drank her tea an hour ago."

Nobody spoke. Maya looked at the table.

"It could be nothing," she said at last. "A name on a building."

"It could. A man signs a lease, and the building outlives him." He pushed the folder an inch toward Arjun. "But I walked up there because of a plant I couldn't explain, and the woman who opened the door wasn't surprised to see me. She knew my name before I gave it."

Maya stood. She took her jacket from the chair back, though she had not taken it off.

"The old file's in the basement archive," she said. "It isn't mine to pull."

"I know," said Ron.

She was already buttoning the jacket.
