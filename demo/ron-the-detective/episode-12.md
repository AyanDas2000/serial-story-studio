# Episode 12: Two Needles, One Error

Draft, not approved.

Ron sat up with the two compasses until the window went gray. He had promised himself he would not tell the story whole, so he told it in pieces and gave each piece a label.

A surveyor's compass, first, carried for years and corrected by hand. That was imagining, he wrote on the back of an envelope, and he put a line under the word. A gift from one man to another, passed across a table, and a deviation kept as a private joke. A thing taken from a house and carried into a yard. He could see each of them clearly, and each had a thumb-bright patch and a worn lid. None of them had a witness.

By the time Maya came at eight, the envelope was covered with possibilities, and Ron had struck through most of them.

"I ran the description last night," she said, setting her bag on his table. "Not the case, just the object. Brass pocket compass, hairline crack across the lid from the hinge. The property index kicks out theft reports by item, so I put in what you told me the woman said." She laid a printout beside the two compasses. "One hit."

He read it. A break-in at a private premises, a list of items taken, and the report closed unsolved. Line six read *one brass pocket compass, hairline crack across lid from hinge.* Beneath it was a date.

"Weeks before the night in the yard," Maya said. "I checked it twice. I'm not saying it's connected. I'm saying a thing that matches her father's compass was reported stolen, and nobody was ever charged."

"The crack matches. That's all the report gives?"

"That's all. There's no serial, and no mention of the needle." She looked at the cloth. "Anyone could have written 'cracked brass compass' about a hundred of them."

"Anyone could." But he picked up the woman's compass and carried it to the window, and tilted the lid to the new light. Inside, near the hinge, the brass was a shade smoother than the rest, in a patch about the size of a thumbnail. He took Maya's pocket lens and bent over it.

The arrival of Arjun and the woman interrupted him. Arjun had come to the door with her, one hand lightly at her elbow, the way he steered clients through lobbies. She had called him herself, she said, after a night of lying awake.

"I looked up what I could," Arjun said when they were sitting. "I want to say this to you plainly, because you didn't come to us expecting it. If that item is the one on the report, you aren't a thief. Your father may have bought it, been given it, or found it. But stolen property doesn't change hands cleanly, and possession of it now is something a prosecutor could call knowing, or at least careless. You'd be asked where it came from. You'd have no answer, and your father can't give one."

She had gone pale around the mouth. "I only wanted to know why he kept it."

"I understand. That's a good reason to have come." He said it kindly. "But you should know where this goes. Sergeant Brandt can't pretend she hasn't seen the report. The thing may have to be handed over, and you may be spoken to."

"Then take it," the woman said. "I don't want it in my house."

"Not yet," Ron said. They looked at him. "If it goes into an evidence room today, it sits there. If it stays with me, with Maya's knowledge and Arjun's paper to say so, I can find out what it is before anyone decides what it was." He turned to Maya. "I'm asking you to let it stay."

Maya was slow to answer. "I can log that I've seen it and that it's in a lawyer's hands," she said at last. "That's the narrowest thing I can do and still be honest. A few days. No longer."

"A few days."

Arjun was already writing out a receipt. The woman signed it with a hand that shook slightly, and then she stood, looking at the brass on the cloth as if it might say something.

Ron had not put the lens down. He had been looking at the smooth patch inside the lid while they talked, and now he held it very still.

"Maya," he said. "Come here."

She bent beside him. Under the glass, in the polished place, the brass was not smooth at all. There were faint, hair-fine scratches, and they had been worn almost level, as if a thumb or a cloth had gone over them for years. A short line. Beside it, a smaller one.

He did not need to fetch the other compass. He knew the mark as he knew his own handwriting.

"Somebody tried to take it off," he said quietly. "And somebody kept rubbing it, after."
