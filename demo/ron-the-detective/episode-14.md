# Episode 14: What the Needle Remembered

Draft, not approved.

The flat was on the second floor of the old ferry terminal, in a wing that had been converted to rooms for staff and later sold off. The woman let them in at four. She stood aside in the bedroom doorway while Ron looked for the place where the table had been.

It was easy to find. Four pale squares on the floorboards, under the window, marked where the legs had stood for years while the rest of the floor darkened. Moss walked the room once and lay down across the threshold.

Ron set the brass compass on a folded cloth in the middle of the pale patch and put his plastic one beside it. Maya waited at his shoulder with the protractor. The needles shivered and stopped, and they parted by the same small angle they had shown at his kitchen table.

"Same," Maya said.

"Same here and same there. So the room isn't doing it." He ruled a line from the window across the floor, as he had ruled one across a yard. It ran into the wall at the head of where the bed had stood. "And I was about to tell you that this is where it points. Which is exactly what I did with the wall outside."

"Then don't."

"I won't. I'll say only that I've got a line that ends in a wall, again."

By six the light had gone, and the woman had gone home to her children with a promise to call. Ron stayed on, sitting on the floor with his back against the radiator. He turned the brass lid over in the lamp's light, and the thumb-bright patch caught it. Maya went down to the car for the street map. A light came on in the corridor, and a trolley rattled on the boards outside.

It was a woman in a grey tabard, pushing a cart with a mop bucket and a rack of folded cloths. She stopped at the open door. She looked at Ron on the floor, at the dog across the doorway, at Maya's badge when Maya came back up, and she did not seem alarmed. "The family said somebody might come," she said. "I do nights here. Terminal and the old flats. Is something wrong with the room?"

"Something's wrong with this," Ron said, and held up the compass. "It points a few degrees off true. I've been trying to work out why."

"Oh, that." She came in and glanced at it, then at the wall. "Everything does that up here. It's the iron."

"Which iron?"

"The building's all frame, the old kind. Girders in the walls, columns on every landing, rails along the stairs." She rapped the wall beside the bed with a knuckle, and it gave back a dull ring. "I've worked nights eleven years. Keys on my belt jump toward the east stair. My cart's got a tray for pins, and the pins stand up like hair if I hold it by the column. We're told not to leave watches on the landings, because they stop. Anything with a bit of steel that lives against the frame for a long while gets pulled, and it holds it. You take it home and it's still pulled."

Ron did not move. "Held it. Even away from the building."

"My husband's pocket knife did. Wouldn't leave it by the girders, it lifted tacks. It does it in the garden shed too. A needle's only a bit of steel," she said, nodding at the compass, "and it will take it worse than a knife, I should think."

Maya looked at Ron. Ron looked at the needle, which leaned its few degrees, patient as ever.

"Thank you," he said. "That's the best thing anyone's told me this week."

When she had pushed the cart on down the corridor, the room was very quiet. Maya set the map on the bare boards.

"Say it," she said. "Out loud, with the labels."

"I called the error a message. I said someone set the needle off true on purpose, so a person who knew could read a bearing off it." He put down the lid. "I made that up. There's no bearing. A needle that has sat against a frame like this long enough carries the frame's pull in it. The few degrees aren't pointing at anything. They're a mark of where it has been. I took a stain for a signature."

"Seen and imagined," Maya said gently.

"Seen: both needles lean the same way, to a hair, and that's what the cleaner can't explain. One needle might have sat here for years. The widow's lay in a yard for a night and in a tin for twenty years, nowhere near this building." He sat forward. "Unless it was somewhere like this first."

"Or both were kept together somewhere, before they were split."

"That I don't know. I'd only be guessing, and I've done enough of it." He took the crumpled printout out of his jacket, the theft report with the cracked compass on line six. "But this had a premises on it. Maya, what's the address of the break-in?"

She took it from him and read the top of the page twice before she answered.

"It's a street, not a building I know," she said. "There's a note beside it: *former customs office.*"
