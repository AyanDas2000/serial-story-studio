# Episode 11: A Few Degrees Wrong

Draft, not approved.

The woman arrived on the stroke of the hour she had given on the phone, and she stood on Ron's step holding her handbag in both hands, like something she meant to keep dry. She was in her forties, in a good raincoat, with the careful posture of someone who has rehearsed a short speech and fears it will come out long. Moss got up from the hall rug, looked at her, and lay down again with his back against her shoe when she sat.

"My father died in the spring," she said. "I've been clearing his flat since, a room at a time. I left the bedroom for last." She opened the bag and took out a cloth bundle and unwrapped it on Ron's table. "This was on his bedside table. Always. Nobody was allowed to move it."

It was a brass compass, round and about the size of a pocket watch. The lid had been dropped or struck at some point, and a hairline crack ran across it from the hinge. The brass was dull everywhere except one patch near the catch, polished bright by a thumb.

"He wasn't a walker," she said. "He wasn't a sailor either. He never went anywhere he'd need it. When I asked, he said it was a keepsake and changed the subject." She hesitated. "I opened it after the funeral, out of curiosity. The needle doesn't point north. I checked it against my phone, and then against the compass on my neighbor's boat key ring. It's out by a few degrees, always the same direction. My father was exact about everything. He wouldn't keep a clock that ran slow. So why would he keep this?"

Ron did not touch it yet. He looked at the lid, the thumb-bright patch, and the crack, and he felt the old pull begin in him, a story assembling itself out of three facts and a wish. A man keeps a faulty instrument beside his bed. A faulty instrument has a reason. He thought of a stiff brass hinge, a scratched mark inside a lid, a woman in a gray cardigan telling him to go on and want it.

He stopped. He made himself say it aloud instead. "I could give you five explanations in as many minutes, and every one would sound true. Please don't believe any of them. I'd like to test the plain thing first. Is it the compass that's wrong, or the needle's surroundings?"

"I don't follow."

"Iron near a needle drags it. A steel table leg, a belt buckle, a magnet in a speaker." He cleared the table of everything but the cloth. "Your phone is in your bag?"

She nodded and put it, at his request, on the sill across the room. He took his own compass, a cheap plastic one from a drawer, and set it on the cloth beside the brass one. He looked at both needles until they stopped shivering. They parted by a small, definite angle, exactly as she had said.

"Does he have a case?" Ron asked. "Something it lived in? A velvet bag?"

"Just the table. A pale wood top, no metal."

"Then your phone and my plastic one agree, and your father's disagrees with both." He sat back. "It's a faulty instrument, or a faulty one made deliberately. Instruments sometimes carry a correction, a small known error that its owner learned to allow for."

"Allow for?" Her voice caught. "Why would he need that?"

"I don't know yet. That's the true answer, and the only one I'll sign."

He went down the hall to the bedroom, where a drawer held the envelope Maya's old widow had given him, gone brown at the folds. He had not shown it to anyone since the flat, and he had told himself it was not the time. He carried it back and, with his back half turned to her, tipped the second compass onto the cloth beside the first.

The woman looked from one to the other. "Where did you get that?"

"From a case I'm not finished with." He lined the two cases up, an inch apart, the thumb-bright catch of one against the worn smooth edge of the other, and waited for both needles to settle.

They settled together, a few degrees from true, and both leaned the same way. The angle did not differ by so much as a hair's width.

Neither of them said anything. Moss lifted his head from the rug.

"Mrs.," Ron said, and then stopped, because he did not know how to go on without a name he had never asked for. He started again. "Would you leave this with me overnight? And tell me everything your father ever said about where he got it."
