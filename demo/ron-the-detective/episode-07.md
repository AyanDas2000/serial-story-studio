# Episode 7: Two Memories of One Night

Draft, not approved.

They walked because Maya said she wanted the air, and because neither of them wanted to sit in a car with the other. Moss kept to Ron's left knee, so close that his shoulder brushed the cloth of Ron's trousers at every step. The streets were wet and nearly empty. Headquarters was five blocks off, and neither of them mentioned it.

"Start with the table," Maya said.

"You weren't at the table."

"No. I was in the corridor with a clipboard, a junior with nothing to do. Start there, and I'll tell you where I was standing when you say something I remember differently."

Ron was quiet for a block. "It was a small kitchen. He wouldn't touch the pen. I remember being very calm, and I remember that the pieces came to me faster than I could say them. The door, the hour, the way a man would have crossed the yard. I could see it, Maya. I didn't feel I was guessing. I felt I was reading a page that was already written."

"And the third man?"

"He came last, and he came easily. Someone had to have been the other pair of hands, and I saw him in the yard as clearly as I see you."

"That's what I remember." She stopped under a streetlamp. "You came out of that kitchen and you were lit up. You'd been in there two hours. You said, 'He was there, it can only be him.' I asked how you knew."

"I don't remember you asking."

"I asked twice. The second time you said I'd understand when I read the statement." She kept her eyes on the wet pavement. "I read it. The third man's part was the only part with nothing under it. No witness, no log, no phone record. It was a thing you saw. I told you I couldn't stand behind that."

"You didn't say that."

"I said it in the corridor, in about those words. You told me I would."

Ron stood still. Moss, pressed against him, stood still too. He tried to find the corridor and could not. He found the kitchen, bright and exact, and the long pull of the story as it ran out of him. He found the afterward in the hearing room, where he had looked at Maya across a table and she had looked at her hands.

"At the hearing," he said, "you said very little."

"I was asked if I'd seen the third man or anything that placed him. I said no. That was all they asked, so it was all I said." Her voice was even, and it took some effort. "I've gone over it a hundred times. I told myself that was honest. Now I think it was only narrow."

"It was true."

"A true answer to the wrong question." She started walking again. "You're not the only one who's good at making a shape out of what's left."

They reached the foot of the headquarters steps. A light burned over the door, and behind the glass a night clerk was bent over a desk. Maya looked up at the building and did not climb.

"I can't go down there tonight," she said. "Not for that file. If I pull it for you, it's mine, and anything it says is tainted, and Arjun will tell you the same. If it's going to mean something, it has to be asked for properly."

"Then I'll wait."

"You're going to want to tell me what's in it." She looked at him sidelong. "I can hear you wanting to. You've already built the box in your head. You know how the pages go."

It was a harsh thing to say, and it was accurate. Ron felt it land. He had been doing it since the lease. He had been rebuilding the old case, the widow's flat, the orchid and the greenhouse all together, a single clean structure with himself at its center, and he had been telling it to himself so smoothly that he'd taken it for memory.

"I'm doing it again," he said. "Telling it whole."

"Yes."

"I'll stop. If I say something tonight, I'll tell you where it came from."

"All right."

"What I know is what I saw at the table, which was my own certainty. What I know about you is what you've just said, which I can't recall." He looked down at Moss, who had not moved from his knee. "I've been thinking that old man withdrew the statement because it was wrong. It might be that he withdrew it because I had made him say it, and the wrongness was only where it showed."

Maya did not answer that. She put a hand into her jacket, then stopped, and he watched her think.

"I never gave the panel my notes," she said. "From that night. They asked for my report and I gave them my report. The notebook is in a shoebox in my closet, and no one has ever asked for it."

"What's in it?"

"I don't remember. I only remember writing." She turned from the steps. "Come on. It's twenty minutes by bus."

Moss was up and walking before Ron had decided to follow.
