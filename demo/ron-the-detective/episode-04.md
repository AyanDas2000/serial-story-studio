# Episode 4: What the Wheel Was Set To

Draft, not approved.

The student came up the service stairs a little after ten, backing onto the roof with a hand truck of seedling trays and a green apron tied over a university sweatshirt. He stopped at the sight of the tape across the door. Moss, sitting on the gravel, looked at him once and went back to watching the stairwell. The student crouched to scratch the dog's ears before he seemed to notice that nobody had told him to.

"I'm from the plant service," he said, standing. "I do the deliveries on the side. Is this the wrong day?"

Ron, who had come out for air, looked at Maya, and Maya looked at the trays. "Which day were you supposed to come?" she asked.

"Today. Thursday's the drop." He glanced past her into the long glass room and the brown ruin along the benches, and the color left his face. "Oh. What happened to them?"

"We're working on that," Maya said. "You've been up here before."

"Most weeks since September. I carry stock up, take dead trays down." He kept looking at the ferns. "It's always the same, though. I pull the pin first."

Ron had been turning toward the door. He stopped. "Which pin?"

"On the timer, the box by the tank." The student pointed with his chin. "It's a wheel with little pins you push in for the times you want. I only ever see two set. If the misters come on while I'm hauling trays, they soak the whole shipment, and the service docks me. So I check the wheel when I get here. Mist runs before dawn, two mornings a week, and I always come on a day when the pins are well away from the hour."

"Two mornings," Ron said. "Not every morning."

"The tech says more than that and the benches go moldy. Two."

"And the morning before you were last here?"

The student frowned, working it out. "I came Tuesday afternoon, the one before this. I looked at the wheel because I always do. The next pin was Wednesday, I think, before sunrise. Then nothing until the weekend." He stopped. "Is that the day it happened?"

Nobody answered. Maya had gone very still with her notebook open. Behind her the owner had come to the door with Arjun, and Ron saw the owner's mouth move without sound.

"Show me the box," Ron said.

They went to the tank room, and the student pointed without touching. It was a round dial the size of a saucer, with a ring of brass pegs around its rim. Two pegs stood out from the rest. The wheel had stopped at a place well short of either one. Ron crouched and followed the arrow on the hub to the mark it rested against, then counted pegs both ways with his finger an inch from the dial.

"The pegs say the mist ran on a morning when it wasn't due," he said slowly. "Or the wheel was turned. Or somebody threw the manual switch." He looked at the switch, a gray lever under a cracked cover, and he did not touch that either. "Did you see it set like this on Tuesday?"

"Same pegs. Same places. I can't swear to the hour on the dial. I only swear to the pegs."

"That's enough," Ron said, and meant it.

When the student had been thanked, and Maya had taken his name from the service sheet and let him carry his trays down again, Ron stood in the middle aisle with the gray crust on every pane around him. He felt the plain hole where an hour before there had been a clean theory.

"I told you the habit killed him," he said. "A man doing his rounds, bolt thrown, mist on its timer. It was a tidy picture, and I liked it because it needed nobody. I was so pleased that it explained the door that I never asked whether the mist was due."

"You asked who signed the log," Maya said.

"I asked the wrong person the right question. The plants died of the fog, I still believe that. The room was sealed, that holds too. But it didn't run itself, not on that morning." He turned to the owner. "Who can reach that switch? Who has a key to the plywood door?"

The owner opened his mouth, shut it, and looked at Arjun. Arjun looked at Ron and, for once, said nothing about privilege.

"Then I start again," Ron said. "The mist was run by someone, on purpose, on the wrong morning. And someone wanted it to look as if it had run itself."

On the far bench the orchid stood in its clean ring. Ron went down the aisle to it, because it was the one thing in the room that had never fit, and he looked at it as if it had just spoken.
