# Episode 10: What the Gate Said

Draft, not approved.

The widow opened the door before Ron knocked. She looked at Maya, at the dog, and at the form in Arjun's folder, which Ron carried, and she said, "Two chairs, then. Good."

She took the newspaper off the second chair and folded it into a drawer. Maya sat in it. Ron stood until she told him to sit, and he took the stool by the stove. Moss lay across his feet.

"Ask your question," she said. "About the gate."

"There was a chain through it, wrapped twice, with the padlock on the yard side. A baker hung bread on that gatepost every morning at ten to four. He says it was never open, that night or the morning after. He told us this morning." Ron kept his hands on his knees. "In my account a man crossed the yard from the lane. I never asked how he got in."

"No," she said. "You didn't."

"Your husband chained it?"

"Every night, for years. He liked a thing shut." She poured tea for the three of them, slowly. "I'll tell you the rest, since you've come properly. A man was in our house that evening. He ate at our table. You had him right, Mr. Calder, though you didn't know it. He was there, and he had a hand in it. He left by the yard door, and my husband let him out into the dark and walked him to the shed. The turn you described, by the shed, is where they stood."

Maya's pen stopped. Ron felt something he could not at first name. It was not relief.

"He didn't come from the lane," Ron said.

"He never came from the lane. He came to supper, by the front. You put his feet on the wrong ground, and everything else was in the right place." She looked at him steadily. "The door, the hour, the two of them in the yard. You said it as if you'd been under the table. My husband sat listening, and I watched him go gray, because it was true. He signed. He told me after that it was like hearing his own mind read out by a stranger, and he hadn't the strength to stand against it."

"And the gate?"

"He lay awake over it that week. He saw that the lane story couldn't be, because of the chain. He saw that a man who couldn't have come over the wall, or through the gate, would be asked how he came, and that the answer was the front door and my table, and that this would lead to us." She turned her cup a quarter turn. "So he withdrew it, and he told them the statement was wrong. He wouldn't say why. That was his pride. He wouldn't be the man who said the gate was chained, because it would have saved a stranger and shamed his own house. He was ashamed of all of it, and he died of that, whatever the certificate says."

Nobody spoke. The radio on the sill had gone to a quiet piano.

"I'm not telling you it was your doing," she said. "You made a true thing out of what you could see. You made one false step in it, and he took the false step as the reason to unsay the whole." She almost smiled. "That's the shape of a clever young man. He's right so far, and he can't hear the one thing that's wrong."

"I was sure," Ron said.

"You were. You still are, a little, I think. But you came here asking about a gate."

He took out the form and laid it on the table with Arjun's pen. "The department never closed the matter. It needs someone from his estate to ask for the file, so that whatever's in it is read. I can't ask. I'm a stranger to it."

She looked at the pen a long time. Then she signed, in a hand that was neat and unhurried, and pushed the page back.

"Wait," she said. She went to the bed, crouched, and drew a tin from beneath it. Out of the tin she took a small envelope gone brown at the folds. "When the police gave back his things, this was in with them. It was picked up in the yard, by the shed, the morning after. I asked who it belonged to, and nobody could say. He wouldn't look at it. I've kept it twenty years because I couldn't throw it out."

She tipped it into Ron's palm. It was a round brass case no bigger than a pocket watch, the lid worn smooth at the edge, the hinge stiff. He thumbed it open. Under cracked glass a needle swung, shivered, and settled. On the inside of the lid a mark had been scratched, a short line and a smaller one beside it, that he did not know.

"Is it his?" Ron asked.

"It wasn't his, and it wasn't mine." She closed his fingers over it. "Which leaves the man who stood by the shed. You'll want to know whose it is, Mr. Calder. I can see it. Go on and want it."

Maya had said nothing. She leaned over now and looked at the scratched lid, and then at him.

Ron looked down at the needle, which pointed steadily at the wall.

"Maya," he said, "bring the notebook to the clerk today. All of it."

"And you?"

"I'm going to find out who carried this."
