# Episode 9: The Missing Stamp

Draft, not approved.

Arjun came to Ron's kitchen at eight, still in yesterday's coat. He set a single photocopied page on the table and laid his hand flat on it, as if it might lift off.

Ron had not slept, and neither had Maya, who sat at the end of the table with the green notebook closed in front of her. Moss lay under Ron's chair with his chin on Ron's shoe.

"You asked me last night to find a door that didn't run through Maya," Arjun said. "I spent the morning in the records office. They keep an index of every hearing the department holds, and the index is public. I looked up yours."

"And?"

"It's listed. The hearing was held. The withdrawal of the statement is filed, and so is the panel's note that it would take no action against you because you'd already resigned." He turned the page toward them and put a finger beside a blank box near the bottom. "This is where a closing memorandum gets stamped and initialed. It's empty. Nobody ever closed the matter."

Maya leaned in. "That can't be right. Somebody would have caught it."

"Somebody should have. The man died, and I think the whole thing was treated as ended by that. But a matter that was never closed is still a matter. The clerk confirmed it, and she took some pains to say she was only reading me the index." He sat. "It means the file doesn't belong to the basement. It belongs to an open question. Someone with standing can ask for it formally. The department then has to produce the whole file, and every note made by every officer who worked it."

Ron looked at the notebook. Maya did not.

"Every note," she said.

"That's what the rule says, and that's the trouble." Arjun said it carefully. "Sergeant, you told us you gave the panel your report and not the notebook. If you hand the notebook in now, on your own, that's a late disclosure. It's uncomfortable, and it may cost you a reprimand. If the request goes in and a clerk is told to collect everything on the matter, and yours turns up in a shoebox, or doesn't turn up, that is a different conversation. They'll ask why it took so long. They'll ask what else you didn't offer."

"I narrowed my answers," Maya said. "I'll say so."

"You'll say it to people who decide your next ten years."

"I know what I'll be saying it to." She put the heel of her hand against her eye. "Ron. Don't look at me like that. I'm not asking you to decide for me."

"I think I have to," Ron said. "That's what's wrong with it. It's my case. I wrote the thing that fell apart, and now I'm choosing how much of it lands on you." He pulled the page toward himself. "If I leave it shut, I've got a baker who says a gate was chained, and a line in your handwriting that says the same. That's something, but it's nothing anyone has to look at. If I open it, they have to look. And then everyone who stood in that corridor is on the record."

"You mean me."

"I mean you."

Nobody spoke. Under the table Moss shifted and settled again.

"Then I'll say the part you can't," Maya said. "I handed in a clean report and kept a page that contradicted your statement because I told myself it wasn't my place. That's the thing I did. The old man signed because you talked him into it, and I let it stand because I didn't want to be the junior who made trouble. If the file opens and it costs me my stripes, then it costs them. I'd rather lose them for this than keep them for that."

Ron did not answer at once. He found he was looking at her hands, which were steady, and thinking of a hearing room where they hadn't been.

Arjun drew a form from his folder and a pen from his pocket and set both in front of Ron. It was a plain pen, black, and Ron looked at it for a long moment.

"Is that it?" he asked. "Sign, and it begins?"

"Nearly." Arjun hesitated. "There's a line here for the requesting party's interest in the matter. It has to be someone with standing, a party to the original proceeding or the estate of one. You resigned before the hearing ended, Ron. You were the officer, not the party. The department would say you're a stranger to it."

"Then who?"

"The man whose statement it was." Arjun looked at the page. "Which leaves his estate."

Ron picked up the pen. He held it a moment, then laid it across the form, as another man once had not been able to. He thought of a gray cardigan buttoned wrong, a chair with a newspaper on its seat, a woman who had said she would say nothing more until he decided which thing he wanted.

"It isn't mine to sign," he said. "It never was."

Maya almost smiled. "Then you'd better go and ask her."

"About the gate," Ron said, and stood.
