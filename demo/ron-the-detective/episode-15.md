# Episode 15: What the Father Kept

Draft, not approved.

The former customs office was a flat brick building with iron-framed windows and a sign for self-storage over the door. The site manager met them in the loading bay in a fleece with the company's name on it. He had a ring of keys on his belt, and he held up the one he wanted before Maya finished explaining.

"The ledger," he said. "You're the third person this year to ask about something from the old days, and the first with a badge. Come up."

Moss went up the stairs at Ron's knee. On the first landing the manager stopped beside an iron column as thick as a man's waist and nodded at a board bolted to it. It was a plain plank with two rows of brass hooks, a number stamped beside each. Most hooks were empty. Beside every hook, scratched small into the wood, was a short line and a smaller one.

Ron stood very still.

"Store mark," the manager said. "When the place was converted we found this board and a ledger in a desk drawer, and nobody had the heart to bin either. Everything the office owned got that mark, scratched in with a nail. Lamps, stamps, rulers. So nobody could walk off with it and sell it."

"Compasses?" Maya asked.

"Two, it says. Hooks nine and ten." He tapped the board. "They hung there for years, bolted to that column. I wouldn't put a watch on this landing."

Ron took his plastic compass out and held it flat against the plank. The needle dragged toward the iron and settled a few degrees off true. It leaned the same way as the two brass ones in his pocket, in the bag he had been carrying since morning.

"That's your lean," Maya said quietly.

"That's where it was made." He lowered the compass. "Not a message. Years of hanging beside that column, and then they carried it with them."

The manager opened the ledger on a packing crate. It was a long, narrow book, ruled in blue, and the entries ran in a small upright hand. Hooks nine and ten were entered together, *issued, night officers, quay round*, with the initials of whoever took them each shift. For years one set of initials appeared against hook nine every night. Against hook ten the initials changed and eventually smudged, as if a wet thumb had passed over them. The last entry for hook nine was a line of different ink: *Reported taken, break-in. Not recovered.* It carried the same date as Maya's printout.

"Whose are the initials on nine?" Ron asked.

"I can't tell you more than the book does. I wasn't here." The manager shrugged. "But the break-in's in there too. Nothing else was reported missing. No cash, no stamps. Odd, I always thought, for a thief to take the one compass."

Ron did not answer at once. He took the woman's father's compass from the bag and held it beside the scratch marks on the board. The ledger's hand, the mark and the hook were all in front of him, and for once he left the empty places empty.

"I can say what the book shows," he said finally. "Hook nine's officer walked the quay for years. The compass was reported stolen on the night the office was entered, and no one was ever charged. Her father had it beside his bed, and the store mark had been rubbed from the inside of the lid for years afterward. That's seen. The rest is my guess, and I'll call it one: that he took it himself, and reported it stolen so the book would show it gone. I think he carried it every night for years, and the office was closing. I think he couldn't bear to hand it back."

"Taking it is still theft," Maya said.

"Yes. A small one, a long time ago, from a place that no longer exists." He looked at her. "I won't know more than that. The man who could say is dead."

Maya looked at the ledger a while, then at the board. "The report's closed unsolved. I can log that the item was identified as property of a dissolved office, no complainant, nothing to recover for. That's honest, and it ends it."

"It stays with her?"

"It stays with her."

Arjun's receipt was in Ron's pocket. He tore it in half in the stairwell and gave the halves to Maya, who folded them into her notebook.

At the woman's door that evening he put the brass compass into her hands. He told her what the ledger said and what it did not say. She turned it over and found the rubbed patch with her thumb.

"He wasn't going to throw it away," she said.

"No. I think that was all of it."

She closed it and held it against her coat.

Back in his kitchen, Ron wrote it up in his notebook, every line labeled seen or guessed. Under *seen* he put the hook board, the ledger and the mark. He wrote the second brass compass under it. The widow's compass carried the same mark and leaned the same way, so it had hung on hook ten, or its twin. Someone had walked it out of that building and into a yard.

Then he turned back to the ledger entry, which he had copied by hand. The initials against hook ten, blurred by a thumb, began and ended with the same letters as the signature on a lease.

He underlined *guessed* and wrote nothing beneath it.
