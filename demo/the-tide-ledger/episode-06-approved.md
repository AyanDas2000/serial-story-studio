# The Tide Ledger, episode 6

Approved prose from the existing author run.

The dry wall had stood where Sef counted it. At first light they had walked it with the Old Cut sucking at the stones on their left, and he had stopped at every landmark with his lips moving, and nothing had been missing but a handrail. Afterward he had gone back to his lock-house without saying what it proved. That was the lockkeeper's way, and Iona understood it. A walked route proved only that a wall existed. It did not prove the leaf had been written the winter it claimed.

For that she needed something that did not belong to her office.

The Harbour Board kept its printed tide books in the public reading room at the Customs Library, where anyone could ask for them and sign a slip. Iona asked for the winter of the Surge and then, because she was careful, for two other winters as well. Neri sat beside her with the level case between her boots, reading the room's rules off the wall as though they were a bench-mark list.

"You've not told me why this matters," Neri said, very low.

"I've told you it's tied to something in the Flood Office."

"That isn't why." But Neri let it go, the way she had let it go at Gate Nine. Iona felt the small cost of it, like a coin spent from a purse that was running low.

The librarian brought the volumes on a trolley. Iona kept the oilcloth tin at home. In her coat she carried only Neri's tracing, folded in quarters. She laid it flat beside the first book and ran her finger down the left margin of the copy, where the original had small pencilled figures she had taken for scratches. Under Neri's ruler they resolved into times: L.W. 4.10, L.W. 4.52, L.W. 5.31, each against a date.

"Low water," Neri said at once. "Those are tide times. Somebody was checking the ebb."

"Against what?"

"Against whether the wall was walkable." Neri turned pages with the delicacy she gave her field book. "Here. The fourteenth, low water 4.10. Fifteenth, 4.52." She stopped. "Sixteenth, 5.31."

"All three agree."

"All six agree. To the minute." Neri sat back. "But anyone could copy that. It's printed."

"I know." Iona had seen it too, and had been holding her disappointment still the way one holds a full cup. "Go to the last date. The night of the Surge."

The printed table for that night showed low water at 2.40, as predicted. The leaf had a different time, written small in the margin and half lost where the damp had crept in: 3.12.

Neri's finger went to it. "That's wrong."

"The sea was wrong that night. The surge held the ebb back." Iona drew the second book across, the Harbour Board's supplement of observed levels, printed in the spring after. It gave low water as 3.15. Gauge-keepers rounded to the quarter hour. "The printed supplement says three-fifteen. The leaf says three-twelve."

Neri compared the figures. "It wasn't copied directly from this table. That helps. It doesn't date the leaf."

"The writer may have used a watch or another observation record. The times fit that winter. We need to check the source."

"Yes. Someone could have checked the gauge house later." Neri examined the hurried figures in her tracing. "And the thread is yours."

"The thread is the office's."

"Same thing, to whoever tore it out."

The librarian came by with her trolley, glanced at the open supplement, and asked whether Iona would want the other winters kept aside. Iona said no, they could go back. The woman nodded and looked at the slip. "Name for the register?"

Iona thought of Sef's good hand clenched on the edge of his desk. "Vale. Flood Office. I'm checking surge times for the sealing survey." The first part was true and the second was the sort of lie that would be unremarkable on any clerk's slip. She wrote it and handed it over, and felt the name enter a record she did not control.

Outside, on the library steps, the wind smelled of tar from the quay. Neri tucked the tracing into its brass tube and slid the tube into the level case.

"So it's real," she said.

"The route and times fit. That corroborates the leaf; it doesn't prove when it was written." Iona watched the harbour. "Or who wrote it."

"Then who has the book it came from?"

"That's tonight." She had known it since the supplement. The brown-inked heading in the evacuation ledger had been written in a hand she had only glanced at, thin and slanted. The margin figures on the leaf slanted the same way. If it was one hand, one person had written both the route and the list of names. She would have to copy a page of the ledger and put the two side by side, and she had two nights left before the lower stacks were boxed.

"I'll need you outside the Flood Office at ten," she said. "You won't come in. You'll keep the tracing."

Neri's mouth twisted, but she nodded.

Iona went down the steps with the slip's carbon curling in her glove, a small proof that she had asked the question aloud.
