# The Tide Ledger, episode 7

Approved prose from the existing author run.

The inspector came to the Flood Office at four, when the lamps were lit in the upper hall and the clerks had begun to look at the clock. He gave no name. He laid a council card on Iona's desk, face up, the way a man sets down a coin to show he can afford the next one. He wore grey gloves, and he took a long time over the removing of them.

"Clerk Vale. A moment of your time, about the lower stacks."

"They're listed," she said. "Every bay but Nine is signed off. I'll finish tonight."

"Bay Nine is what I'd like to speak of." He opened a leather folder and turned it to her. It held a printed schedule, ruled in the contractors' blue. "You'll know the dates. Boxing closes Friday noon. Perhaps you don't know that the weather office has promised a hard blow from Saturday, so the works have been drawn forward." His finger moved down the page. "Grout is pumped into the vault at the Thursday night low water. Everything left on the shelves stays where it stands. The quay slab goes over it at dawn."

Iona kept her face still, as she did when a flood claimant lied to her. Thursday night. She had counted on Friday noon, and had been hoarding the hours like coal.

"Anything marked of no value is left in place," he went on. "Saves the cartage. I see you marked a good many."

"They were damaged."

"One in particular. Canvas, Low Wards, evacuation." He looked up. "I'm told something was taken from the footing at Gate Nine yesterday. A seam opened, a patch broken out. The foreman recalls a clerk with a Flood Office badge, and a girl with a staff. He did not recall a name." He let that sit. "Any paper found in a council work belongs to the council. I'm asking you to hand it over, today, and sign the certificate that you have no other papers from the lower stacks. Then there is no difficulty."

On his folder lay the certificate, with a space for her name. She thought of her own pencilled line beside the canvas volume, *no value*. She had written it to hide the ledger, and he had just shown her it would bury it.

"I have no girl with a staff," she said. "And I don't sign certificates for papers I haven't counted."

"But you do hold something."

The token lay against her ribs under the coat lining. At home, in a place only she knew, the tin sat with her thumbprint in its wax. She weighed what she could say that was not a lie. She had learned in eleven years that a flat true sentence was harder to open than an elaborate false one.

"If I held such a paper," she said, "it would be sealed under my own hand. It would stay sealed until a magistrate's clerk broke it in front of a witness. That's the rule for evidence of title and I'd follow it for anything found in the stone. Not for you. Not for the contractors. For the clerk."

For the first time the inspector smiled, though it did nothing to his eyes. "You know how that sounds."

"I know how the rule sounds."

"Clerk Vale, the sealing is not a matter of preference. There are three hundred households behind that wall." He drew the certificate back and closed the folder over it. "I won't send a porter to turn out your desk. That would be unseemly, and you're a long-serving officer. But the grout does not wait for sentiment. Until Thursday's low water, the offer stands. After it, there is no vault, and so no paper, and no question to answer."

"And if I'm asked what I told you?"

"You told me nothing. That's rather the point." He pulled on his gloves, one finger at a time. At the door he glanced back at the shelf marks above her desk, and left.

She sat very still until the clerk at the next table lost interest. Then she took out the slip she had folded in her glove three nights ago, with its shelf mark and page number and the words *Cooper's Stair*. The inspector had not known about the slip. He had not known about the tin, or the token, or the Customs Library. He had known only that something had been in the stone, and he had come to her first.

That meant someone had decided her name was worth reading from a foreman's report. She could not tell whether it was a courtesy or the first turn of a screw.

By ten the hall was dark. Neri would be on the corner by the chandler's, keeping the tracing in her level case, unwilling and loyal. Iona would give her a different errand now. She had planned to copy a single page of the ledger, the heading in brown ink and a few names beside her brother's. That was too little. There would be no second night.

She opened her desk drawer, took the clean ream of grey slip paper she used for shelf labels, and counted out forty-two sheets, one for every name.
