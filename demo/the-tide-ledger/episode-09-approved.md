# The Tide Ledger, episode 9

Approved prose from the existing author run.

Iona reached the lock-house in the dark before the dark thinned, and Sef opened the door as before, fully dressed, as if he had not been to bed either. She did not go in at once. Neri was waiting at the head of Tallow Quay with the level case, out of sight of this door by arrangement. Iona had promised her that much.

"I haven't told you whose name was in the ledger," she said.

"I noticed."

"It's Tomas. My brother. Seven years ago he went missing, and the ledger lists him among people never born." She watched his face. "I need to ask you for something, and I'd rather you say no knowing why than yes without."

Sef lowered himself into the lockkeeper's chair. His left hand rested in his lap, curled around nothing. For a while there was only the tide shifting in the stopped lock.

"Ask."

"You said the count went to the Flood Office every night. I think you know more than the count. I think you know what was done with it. If you tell me, I want to carry it to a magistrate's clerk with your name on it." She made herself finish. "That breaks what I promised you. I won't do it unless you free me, and you should know what it could cost. Your pension. This house."

"I know what it could cost." He said it mildly. "I did the sums when you sat down at that desk with your brass. Go on and let me say it, and then I'll tell you whether you may use it."

She sat. She did not take out paper. He would speak more freely to a face than to a pencil.

"The winter of the Surge," Sef said, "the tally book for the Old Cut landing was kept by me, in my hand, every night. Boat numbers, and below them, because it was the evacuation, a head count off each boat. A clerk from your office came on the third morning after. Young man, wet to the knees, with a bundle of printed forms. He called it a transfer."

"A transfer of what?"

"Of residence. The Low Wards were to be struck from the parish rolls for the rebuilding, and every household entered fresh on the Hill register, by name, as they came off the boats. That was the form. Old parish entry cancelled, new entry made. Tidy." His good hand opened and shut. "He wanted my count to match his entries. Where a boat's head count was short of the names on the Hill, that was no matter. Where it was over, there was a line for it at the foot of the form. Persons not otherwise accounted. He had me sign the foot of every sheet, to say the landing had been closed and all counted."

"And you signed."

"I'd been at the gate eighteen hours a day for a week. He said the sheets would be checked against the rolls and any slip put right." Sef's mouth flattened. "I signed. A week later I went to the Hill for my sister's girl and found her entered, spelled wrong, and no trouble. But I heard of others. A man whose parish entry was cancelled, no Hill entry made, and when he went to the office for his paper they said there was no record he had ever been born. Not lost. Never. The cancelling had gone through, the new entry never had, and the form was signed, so the office stood on the form."

Iona kept her hands flat on her knees. "Forty-two people."

"I never knew a number. I knew two, and then I stopped asking, because a man who asks at that office is entered himself." He looked at her directly. "Understand what I'm saying and what I'm not. I never saw who wrote the order. The forms bore the Reclamation Committee's stamp, and it had a dozen members. Your Councillor Rusk was one, so was half the council. I couldn't say she read a form. I couldn't say anyone did. What I can swear is the young man, the forms, my signature at the foot, and that the tally book went in a satchel to the Flood Office and was never given back."

"That's a process, then," she said. "Not a person."

"A process has people in it. You'll find them by following it. I'd not hang one on my word." He rubbed the dead hand with the live one, slowly. "Is that enough for your clerk?"

"It's enough to ask where the satchel went."

"Then listen to my terms. I'll swear it before a magistrate's clerk, with a witness, in a room with a door. Not to the council, not to that grey-gloved fellow who came to your desk, and I did hear of him. You'll tell nobody I'm willing until the day, and when the day comes you'll be there."

"I will."

"Then you're free of it." He pushed himself up with the desk's edge. "And this morning I'm walking down Cooper's Stair with you. A man who signed those sheets ought to see the doors."

She had not asked, and she had not known how. Iona stood, and went out to tell Neri there would be three of them.
