# The Tide Ledger, episode 8

Approved prose from the existing author run.

Neri was waiting at the corner by the chandler's, where the lamp had a cracked glass and threw its light in a fan across the cobbles. She had the level case between her boots and her collar turned up. She did not look relieved to see Iona. She looked like someone who had rehearsed a speech and been robbed of its first line.

"You were forty minutes," she said.

"Longer than I wanted." Iona shifted the bundle under her coat. Forty-two grey slips, copied in her smallest hand, every name and address and age, the brown-inked heading at the top of the first. Her fingers were stiff with cold and with effort. "I need these out of the building tonight. They can't sit in my desk. The inspector gave me until Thursday's low water, and after that the vault is grouted."

"Thursday." Neri swallowed it. "You said Friday."

"I was wrong."

She held out the bundle. Neri took it and, before Iona could stop her, tilted it under the lamp to fit it into the case. The top slip was the one Iona had copied last, because she had not been able to bring herself to copy it first. It was only a line of her own handwriting.

Tomas Vale. Eleven Cooper's Stair. Aged nineteen.

Neri read it. Iona watched her read it. Then Neri's eyes went up, slowly, from the slip to Iona's face, and back to the slip.

"Vale," she said.

"It's a common name in the Low Wards."

"It's your name. And Cooper's Stair is on the leaf, forty-one steps down. I traced it myself." Her voice stayed low, but it had an edge now. "You told me you were looking for a number. You let me measure a gate and copy a page and sit in the Customs Library signing for the tide books, and all the time it was your own name in that book."

"It isn't my name. It's my brother's."

Iona let the words stand. For seven years she had said them to clerks and watchmen who could do nothing. Saying them to someone who was helping her felt different.

Neri did not speak. A cart went by with its wheels grinding on the stone.

"Tomas," Iona said. "He's three years younger than I am. He went out one winter and didn't come back, and the harbour watch had nothing, and the parish had nothing. I don't know if he's alive. I found him in that ledger under a heading that says he was never born, and I didn't tell you because I thought you'd stop trusting what I asked you to measure."

"I might have."

"I know."

"I might still." Neri pushed the slip back to the top of the bundle and shut the case, hard. "You should have said it at the gate. I'd have been angry then, and it would have been over by now."

"Yes."

"Does the lockkeeper know?"

Iona felt the question take hold, because it was the one thing she would not give away. "He knows there's a ledger. He knows the token came out of it. I didn't tell him whose name, and he didn't ask." She kept her voice level. "He's the reason the route made sense. If anyone asks you who read it, you read it. You counted the steps from the Stair yourself, from the leaf alone, and the inspector can have your word for it. You don't know a lockkeeper. There's no house on Tallow Quay."

"I never knew one." Neri's mouth twisted. "I was quite good at that, wasn't I? I didn't even have to try."

"I promised him."

"You told me what you promised. I'm not asking you to break it." She shouldered the case. The strap creaked. "But I'm carrying forty-one strangers and your brother across the city in a box for surveying chains, and I'm doing it because I can't get at the archive and you can. So from now on you don't hold anything back because you think it will frighten me."

"It was never that."

"Then what?"

Iona looked at the chandler's dark window, the coils of rope hanging in it like old pale arteries. "I thought if I said it, it would be true," she said. "That he was put on a list of people who were never born, by someone with a pen, on purpose. While I only held the token, it could still be a mistake."

Neri was quiet. Then, with a gruffness that did not quite cover her care, she pulled the strap tighter. "A mistake doesn't tear a page out of the gutter and hide it in a gate."

"No."

"Then the forty-one are real, and the house on the Stair is real. Somebody there will remember them." She jerked her chin toward the black line of the harbour, where the first grey of the sky had not yet begun. "Those were your neighbours, Iona. They'll open a door to you."

Iona had not been up Cooper's Stair in seven years. She had crossed the city to avoid its foot. She thought of the grout, and Thursday's low water, and the forty-two slips pressing against Neri's hip.

"At first light," she said. "I'll go alone."

"You'll go with me at your back, at the bottom of the steps," Neri said. "Where I can see the door."
