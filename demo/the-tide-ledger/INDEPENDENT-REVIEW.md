# Independent review: Episodes 1–10

**Overall verdict: REVISE.** This is a compelling, controlled civic mystery with a credible central relationship and unusually tangible paperwork. The opening ten episodes establish a strong story rather than merely illustrating a premise. Revision is warranted because the destruction deadline loses clarity at the critical overnight transition, the planned authentication remains incomplete, and several evidentiary assertions need firmer support. These are local problems, not grounds to discard the arc.

This review uses only the supplied premise, spine, ten plan lines, and approved episode texts. Word counts below use whitespace-separated tokens; punctuation attached to a word does not create an additional token.

## Prose and dramatic quality

The prose is strongest when the city and Iona's profession shape her decisions. The painted flood band she “no longer saw,” the damaged canvas volume, and the concealment of the token in a loose coat seam establish place, habit, and risk without exposition-heavy worldbuilding. Episode 1's final catalogue entry, “damaged, no value,” becomes especially effective when episode 7 reveals that this designation determines which records will be buried. A defensive lie acquires a material cost.

Dialogue generally has pressure behind it. Sef's “I've a pension that depends on my being a man who forgets” gives his silence an understandable price. Neri's “That isn't why” punctures Iona's technically truthful evasions. The characters have distinguishable priorities: Iona controls information, Neri wants reciprocal trust, and Sef negotiates the terms of exposure.

The narration occasionally explains a distinction immediately after the scene has already demonstrated it. Episode 4's “It's a bell, not a count” reinforces a separation the preceding paragraph and Neri's tide explanation have made clear. Episode 9's “That's a process, then ... Not a person” similarly compresses a richer moral problem into an explicit lesson. These lines could be reduced without losing intelligibility.

The setting stays grounded and within Iona's viewpoint. There is no supernatural shortcut, no independent conspirator scene, and no premature solution to Tomas's fate.

## Continuity, knowledge, custody, and promises

Names and relationships remain stable. Tomas is consistently Iona's missing brother, and his fate remains unknown. His disappearance seven years earlier, his recorded age of nineteen, and Iona being three years older are compatible. Sef's permanently impaired left hand is remembered across handling, tracing, testimony, and walking scenes; his use of it as a shallow cup or support does not amount to a recovery.

Neri's archive restriction is preserved: public library access is different from municipal archive access. She does not learn Tomas's connection until episode 8. She does, however, meet Sef and visit his house in episode 5. Consequently, “You don't know a lockkeeper” and “I never knew one” in episode 8 should be understood as protective cover, not literal knowledge continuity. The twisted mouth and discussion of the promise support that reading. This is a plausible ambiguity, not a definite contradiction, but clearer phrasing would prevent confusion.

Iona's promise protects Sef from the council and its informants, rather than forbidding every introduction. Episode 5 explicitly negotiates that distinction. Episode 9 handles consent particularly well: Iona explains both her purpose and the possible loss of pension and house, and Sef imposes conditions before releasing her. His permission concerns a magistrate's clerk, not unrestricted disclosure. Nothing shown subsequently clearly breaches those conditions.

Custody is mostly legible. The token remains with Iona; the ledger stays in the archive; Neri holds the tracing; the blue-thread original enters Iona's wax-sealed tin and is subsequently described as being at home. The widow retains her receipt after allowing a tracing. The bell, token number, and thread never become interchangeable clues.

The major continuity concern is the clock. Episode 7 advances destruction to “Thursday night low water” and concludes, “There would be no second night.” Episode 8 moves from the ten o'clock rendezvous toward a first-light visit; episodes 9 and 10 conduct that visit, then choose another visit “tomorrow.” Yet the original ledger remains below ground, and the narrative never establishes whether Thursday's low water has passed or whether the ledger has now been destroyed. This is an unresolved timeline and consequence gap, rather than a provable date contradiction: exact tide timing is not supplied. Specify the remaining interval and acknowledge the ledger's fate. Otherwise the central deadline appears to expire between scenes without dramatic reckoning.

## Length, repetition, and strongest episodes

Counts are: **1: 784; 2: 829; 3: 799; 4: 783; 5: 776; 6: 873; 7: 871; 8: 895; 9: 882; 10: 888.** All comply with 650–900 words. The mean is 838, above the 780-word aim; the later episodes cluster close to the ceiling. That is a pacing observation, not a length violation.

There is little outright filler or wholesale recap. Repeated dry mouths, good and dead hands, folded slips, guarded true sentences, and locks closing in faces sometimes become mannerisms. Retain repetitions that track custody or disability; trim repeated emotional shorthand.

**Strongest: episodes 1 and 7.** Their linked “no value” decision gives the mystery consequence and makes bureaucracy an active antagonist. **Weakest: episode 6**, which advances corroboration but ends with another verification still required. Episode 10 is satisfying as a limited procedural proof, although the unnamed widow deserves a name: the first person whose identity the allies restore should feel more individual than “the woman.”

The arc merits continued development after a focused revision of deadline continuity, authentication, and unsupported corroboration.
