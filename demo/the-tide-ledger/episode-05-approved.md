# The Tide Ledger, episode 5

Approved prose from the existing author run.

Iona had talked her way past the foreman with her badge and a lie about checking flood marks before the contractors' tape went up. Neri's sleeve, black to the elbow, had done the rest, because no one suspects silt of anything. By noon they stood on Sef Marr's step with the leaf buttoned inside her coat.

He looked at Neri for a long moment. "I said no one."

"You said not to name you to the council, or to anyone who carries to them," Iona said. "She's an apprentice surveyor who can't get into the archive. She found it, Sef. I'd have lost it to the tide."

"I've never heard your name, Mr. Marr," Neri said, quite steadily. "I can keep that up."

He stood aside.

The leaf would not lie flat. Silt had stiffened it into its old folds, and it curled at the corners when Iona laid it on the lockkeeper's desk. Sef set his left hand on one edge, the curled fingers making a small bridge, and held the other corner down with his thumb. The blue thread trailed across the wood.

"That's your binder's thread," he said, not asking.

"Yes."

He bent over the columns. Iona had studied them since leaving Gate Nine without making sense of them: ward names down the left, then rows of short figures, dates for the winter of the Surge, and the half-eaten heading. Sef read with his lips moving a little, as he must once have read the tally boards.

"This isn't a ledger," he said slowly. "It's a route. Look. Cooper's Stair, forty-one steps down. Dyer's Bridge, that's the footbridge, they took it down after. Then the figure here, nine, is the yards to the Old Cut landing along the dry wall. Eleven, thirty-three, nine." He tapped each with his good thumb. "A man who'd walked it counted it out like this, so a stranger could do it in the dark."

"The evacuation went by the Tallow Road," Iona said. "To the shelters on the Hill. It's in every account."

"When the road went under, the carters used this. Down the Stair, along the dry wall, across to the Old Cut, and boats took them from the landing." He straightened. "Not in any account, because nobody wrote it down. Or somebody did, and took the trouble to tear it out."

Cooper's Stair, forty-one steps. Iona thought of her brother's name in brown ink, and of forty-one other names, and shut the thought away because it would not help her hands.

"I want a copy," she said. "Now, before anything else."

Neri already had thin survey paper out of her case, the kind she used for tracing section lines. She laid it over the leaf and ruled it with a pencil, and Iona read each figure aloud, her finger under it, while Neri drew it exactly: every stroke, every smudge, the ragged edge where the heading had rotted. Sef made them stop twice to correct a nine that Iona had read as a four. When it was done, Neri wrote at the foot the date, the hour, and the state of the tide, as she would for a bench mark, and signed with her own name.

"Not yours," she told Sef. "Nothing of yours."

"Good girl."

The copy went into the brass-capped tube Neri used for plans, then into her level case, among the staves and plumb-lines of a trade nobody searches.

The original was Iona's. She had brought a flat tin from home, the one her mother kept pressed papers in. Now she laid the leaf in a fold of oilcloth, the blue thread curled on top, and fitted the lid down. Sef gave her the stub of a candle from the lock-house drawer. She dripped wax along the seam and pressed her thumb into it, so that anyone who opened it would break her print.

"That goes where?" Sef asked.

"Somewhere only I know."

"Then you'd better be the one who's right about it."

She put the tin inside her coat with the token. It was a little heavier than she had expected.

Neri was staring out of the window at the grey water in the Old Cut, where it slid between the staked and taped banks. "They close that bank tomorrow noon," she said. "Tape, stakes, a gang with a pump. After that nobody walks the dry wall."

Sef was quiet so long that Iona thought he would refuse. Then he took his oilskin from its peg, one-handed, and shook it out.

"Then we walk it at first light," he said. "I want to see if the wall's still where I counted it."
