# The Tide Ledger, episode 1

Approved prose from the existing author run.

The Flood Office kept its oldest records below the harbour line, where the walls sweated in every season and a painted band at shoulder height showed how far the water had come in the Great Surge. Iona Vale had worked beneath that band for eleven years. She no longer saw it.

Tonight she saw nothing else, because the sea wall contractors wanted the lower stacks boxed, listed, and ready to be sealed by the end of the week. After that, the survey notice said, the vault would be filled and the quay built over it. Iona's job was to write one line for each volume. Most took a minute.

The volume on the bottom shelf of Bay Nine was bound in water-stained canvas, and the spine said only LOW WARDS, EVAC. She carried it to the lamp and opened it on the sorting table. The columns were ruled in a clerk's careful hand: name, address, age, boat or cart, destination. Near the back the ruling changed. A heading had been added in a different ink, browner and thinner: Persons not entered in the register of births. Nothing followed it but names.

She read them the way she read everything, with a finger under each line, until the finger stopped.

Tomas Vale. Eleven Cooper's Stair. Aged nineteen.

Her mouth went dry. Cooper's Stair was where they had grown up. Nineteen was what he had been the last winter she saw him. But he had a birth certificate. She had carried it herself to three offices after he vanished, and three clerks had looked at it and nodded. He had a christening cup, a school record, and a scar on his chin from the ferry rail. Someone had written him here as a person who had never been born.

She turned back a page, then forward, and counted forty-one other names in the brown ink. She did not know any of them. She did not let herself think about what that meant.

When she lifted the next leaf, something small slid out of the gutter and rang once against the table.

It was a brass token the size of a thumbnail, worn smooth at the edges, with a hole bored near the rim for a cord. A number was stamped into its face: 47. There was no office mark, no ward crest. She turned it in the lamplight and found a dark crescent of old verdigris on one side, where a thumb had held it for years, or a pocket had.

Above her head the pipes knocked as the tide pushed into the culverts. Iona made herself breathe slowly and think like a clerk. The ledger was catalogued, and its shelf mark was in the register at the top of the stairs. A signed-out volume would be noticed; a missing one would be looked for. If she carried it up to the street she would be a woman stealing a public record on the night before the vault was boxed. If she copied the page here, she would need an hour and a clear conscience, and she had only one of those.

She set the token beside the ledger and tried to be sure it belonged with it. Could it be a coincidence? A chandler's tag, a ferry fare, a laundry check from some forgotten tenant? But it had been pressed into the page that held her brother's name, and the leaf above it was dented where it had lain.

She read the line once more, to be sure of the spelling and the stair. Then she took a sliver of the grey slip paper she used for shelf labels and wrote the volume's shelf mark and the page number, and under them, small, the words Cooper's Stair. She folded the slip twice.

Footsteps crossed the floor above, a night porter's slow circuit, and stopped at the head of the stairs. A key rattled against a ring.

Iona closed the ledger and slid it into its gap on the bottom shelf of Bay Nine, spine out, as though no hand had moved it. The slip went inside her glove. The token she held a moment longer, warm now from her palm. Then she pushed it down into the lining of her coat, beneath the seam where the stitching had been loose for a year.

"Still down there, Clerk Vale?" the porter called.

"Nearly finished," she said. She was surprised at how steady it sounded, and how easily it came.

She picked up her pencil, opened the box list to the line for Bay Nine, and wrote beside the canvas volume: Low Wards evacuation, damaged, no value.

Her hand did not shake until she had signed it.
