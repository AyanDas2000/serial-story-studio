# The Tide Ledger, episode 10

Approved prose from the existing author run.

Cooper's Stair had forty-one steps. Sef counted them under his breath, his stiff hand on the rail. At the thirty-third he stopped to look at the dry wall.

Iona did not look at number eleven. She had told herself she would not, and she passed its green door with her eyes on the next lintel. Neri, at the foot of the steps with the level case, had the view of every door and none of the faces.

The woman they wanted lived on the fourth landing. Iona had picked her from the slips the night before: a name, an address, an age that would now make her past sixty. She came to the door with flour on her wrists and listened to Iona's plain account, standing very straight. When Iona said the words *not entered in the register of births*, she gave a short laugh.

"Write it down," she said. "I've been dead on paper long enough. Come in, and mind the cat."

Her kitchen smelled of damp wool. From a biscuit tin on the dresser she took her papers one at a time and laid them on the table like cards. A christening cup, dented. A school prize with her name in gilt. A letter from the Flood Office, six years old, regretting that no entry of birth could be found in either the parish roll or the Hill register, and that the applicant's claim to a widow's allowance could not therefore be considered. The stamp was the office's own. Iona had inked that stamp herself.

"And this," the woman said, and unfolded last a half-sheet gone brown at the creases. "They gave it me off the boat. A receipt, they said. Keep it safe."

It was a carbon of a printed form: her name, her old parish entry marked *cancelled for transfer*, a blank where the Hill entry should have been, and along the foot, in a clerk's hand, *Persons not otherwise accounted*. Beneath that, a signature in blunt capitals.

Sef bent over it. For a while he said nothing. Then he laid his right forefinger beside the signature, not touching it.

"That's mine," he said. "I made the tail of that R like a gate hook. I did it on every sheet."

Iona set her copied slip beside the receipt: the same name, street and age. She added the letter and christening cup, then read the sequence aloud for the woman.

"She was born. The cup, the school prize, and her neighbours say so. The parish struck her entry for transfer. The Hill entry was never made. Lockkeeper Marr's signature closes the sheet as correct. And years later the Flood Office, which holds the ledger that lists her among the never-born, tells her it can't find her." She stopped. "That isn't a lost paper. A form was completed, and someone relied on it afterward."

"It's the foot of the form that does it," Sef said. "The line for the over-count. They had to put names somewhere."

"Then the list was a drain," Iona said slowly, "not a plan. At least from the landing."

"Perhaps." He did not sound convinced, and neither was she. A drain still had to be dug, and the heading in the brown ink had been added by a hand.

The woman folded her flour-white wrists. "Will it help to have it copied?"

"Yes. Neri, my colleague, will trace it, if you'll allow it," Iona said. "You'll keep the original. I can't promise it's safe in this house."

"Nothing's safe in this house. I've kept it seven years anyway." She hesitated, then tipped her head toward the window. "A man came Tuesday, with grey gloves. He asked had anyone on the Stair ever had a paper from the transfer. Nicely, as if for a survey. I said we'd all lost ours in the flood."

Iona felt it go through her, cold and exact. Tuesday was before the inspector had come to her desk. He had known to ask here before he asked her.

"I'd not have you named as a witness," she said. "Not yet. If a magistrate's clerk asks, then you'll be asked in a room with a door."

"I've told you what I think of being quiet."

"I know. Be quiet a little longer."

With the widow's permission, Neri traced the receipt at the kitchen table and returned it before they left. On the steps, she glanced at number eleven without comment. Sef sat on the thirty-third step.

"The widow can't be the next," he said. "She's been visited. And the lad with the forms is a name I never had."

"The parish kept the cancelled rolls," Iona said. "Every struck entry was written in the parish book before it was struck. If the sexton still has that book, it shows the cancelling without the Hill's help, and without anyone in the Flood Office."

"Old Aldermoor's sexton lives past the Lanterns, on a pension from the parish, not the council," Neri said. "I passed his gate when I lodged. I don't know if the inspector's been there."

"Then we check before we ask him for anything." Iona passed the tracing back for Neri's tube. "We go tomorrow, if the street is clear. And we offer him a way to refuse."

The sexton was their next choice, subject to his consent.
