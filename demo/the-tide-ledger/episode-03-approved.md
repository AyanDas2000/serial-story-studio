# The Tide Ledger, episode 3

Approved prose from the existing author run.

Gate Nine was the last of the old tidal gates before the quay turned toward the open harbour, and the contractors had already staked it. Orange tape ran from post to post across the wet stone. Iona had come because Sef had said the east locks all shared a pattern, and she wanted to see what the council's measuring chain had been measuring.

She had not expected anyone else. But a young woman knelt at the gate's foot with a levelling staff laid beside her, a field book open on one knee, her sleeve black to the elbow where she had been reaching into the silt. She had the tight, careful look of someone trying not to be seen to be worried.

"That's closed ground," the young woman said without looking up.

"So is the lower archive, officially. I work there." Iona held up her office badge. "Flood Office. Iona Vale."

"Neri Pell. Apprentice, Haskett and Dunn." She said it flatly, as one reads out a sentence. She glanced at the badge, then at the street behind Iona, then back at the stone. "I'm not supposed to talk to the Flood Office."

"Why not?"

"Because I'm not supposed to be looking at this." Neri tapped the field book. "It's probably nothing."

"People say that when it isn't."

Neri hesitated, then turned the book round. Two columns of figures, one in pencil, one in ink. "The contractor's plan gives the sill of this gate at two feet one below datum. The mark cut in the stone says three feet four. I've checked it three times with the staff." She drew a finger along the gate's base, where a small arrow had been chiselled into the granite above a line. "That's an old mark, weathered the same as the stone around it. But somebody has cut a second line a hand's width higher and dressed it to match. The plan follows the high one."

Iona crouched. In the angle of the stone she could see it: two lines, one soft with age, one sharper, its edges barely rounded by salt. "So the plan says the sill is shallower than it is."

"By thirteen inches. Which is nothing, until you pour a wall foundation on it. Then it's thirteen inches of something you haven't accounted for." Neri shut the book. "My master says the plan was checked against the levelling book. I can't see the levelling book. Apprentices don't get archive passes. I can read marks. I can't read what's filed."

"And you came back alone to make sure."

"I came back because it bothered me." She said it almost angrily. "Why would anyone move a mark?"

Iona watched the tide lap at the lowest step. She thought of the slip in her glove, and of Sef's hand closing on the desk's edge. Neither would go into this conversation, and she shaped what she would give with the same care she gave a catalogue line.

"I'm looking for something that was written down once and then wasn't," she said. "A number. Forty-seven. It was stamped on a piece of brass, and I don't know what it counted."

Neri's head came up. "Forty-seven what?"

"I don't know yet."

"There's no forty-seven on this line. The bench marks stop at thirty-one." She frowned, pulling the thought apart. "Could be a boat number. A berth. A lot number if somebody sold a plot."

"Could be."

"Whose is it?"

"I can't say," Iona said, and watched Neri accept that, not happily. "I can say it's tied to something in the Flood Office. If you see it anywhere, a stamp, a scratch, a tag on a stake, I'd like to know."

"And what do I get?"

It was a fair question, and Iona answered it plainly. "If I can find the levelling book for this line, I'll tell you whether your mark or the plan is the true one. I can't promise to show you the pages."

Neri looked at her for a long moment, at the badge, the clerk's gloves, the grey bruised look of someone who hadn't slept. Then she tore a page from the back of her field book and wrote on it: a lodging street, a door number, and the hour of the lowest tide tomorrow.

"The contractors close this tape at noon," she said. "Before that, the stone's mine to measure. Bring me the book, and I'll show you where they've been cutting."

Iona took the paper. Above them, a foreman's whistle sounded along the quay, and the tape shivered in the wind.

She folded the page into her glove beside the other slip. The lower stacks would be boxed in four days. She would go back to Bay Nine that night, and this time she would look for a levelling book.
