# The Tide Ledger, episode 2

Approved prose from the existing author run.

Sef Marr lived in the old lock-house at the end of Tallow Quay, where the lock had been stopped with concrete since before the Great Surge's anniversary stones were laid. Iona reached it at first light, with the tide out and the mud shining like the inside of a mussel shell. She had not slept. She had gone home, taken the token from her coat lining, set it on the kitchen table, and watched it until the window greyed.

He opened the door before she knocked. He was a broad man gone narrow, in a fisherman's jersey darned at both elbows, and his left hand hung at his side with the fingers curled halfway to a fist, as it had since she was a girl.

"You've the look of someone with a question," he said. "Come in, the kettle's not going to bring itself."

The front room still held the lockkeeper's desk, its brass fittings dull under years of damp. Iona waited until he had set down two tin cups. Then she took the token from her glove and laid it on the desk between them.

"What is this?"

Sef looked at it without touching it. Something in his face closed, like a lock gate easing shut. "Where's it from?"

"Tell me what it is first."

"That's a poor trade, Iona Vale."

"It's the only one I've got."

He grunted. He reached with his right hand, then stopped and brought the left up beside it, the way a man does from long habit, to steady what he lifts. The token was small and slick. His thumb pinned it to the desk, but the dead fingers would not curl under to lift it. He tried twice. On the second try it skated out from under his thumb and fell, rang on the boards, and rolled toward the stove.

Iona started forward. "Leave it," he said, too sharply. He lowered himself with a hiss, found the token against the iron foot of the stove, and scooped it into the crook of his left hand, where the half-curled fingers made a shallow cup. He did not look at her while he did it. When he stood, he turned the token over with his good thumb until the verdigris crescent faced up.

"Lock tally," he said. "Brass ones, for the tally board. A boat came into the chamber, you hung its number on a hook, and you took it down when she was through. That way you knew what was still inside when the gates shut."

"Tanner's Lock?"

"Tanner's, the Old Cut, every lock on the east side used the same pattern. Same foundry." He rubbed the worn rim. "Only the boards had forty-six hooks. I cut the last ones myself, the winter before the Surge. There was never a forty-seven."

Iona's mouth had gone dry again. "Then it's a fake."

"Or somebody had one made." He set it back on the desk, gently, as if it might still roll. "Where was it?"

She told him: the Flood Office, the evacuation ledger, the leaf with the brown-inked names. She did not say whose name. She heard herself leave it out, and saw by the way he watched her that he heard it too.

"Who else knows?"

"No one."

"Keep it so." He pulled his chair closer. The scrape of the legs was loud. "You'll go asking about this. Of course you will. You'll ask at the lock, at the yards, at the foundry if it's still standing. Sooner or later a councillor's clerk will ask you how you came to know the lock pattern, and you'll want to say."

"I wouldn't."

"Promise me." His good hand closed on the desk's edge. "Not my name, not this house, not that I was lockkeeper. The council has been through the east locks with a measuring chain this month, and there are men who'd rather the old keepers kept quiet until the wall's poured. I've a pension that depends on my being a man who forgets."

"Sef—"

"Say it."

She thought of the slip in her glove, and Cooper's Stair, and the mouth of the harbour where the concrete would go. "I won't name you to the council or to anyone who carries to them. Not you, not the house."

He let out a breath. Some of the stiffness went out of his shoulders, though not out of the hand. He pushed the token across to her with the flat of his right palm.

"Then I'll tell you the rest. The tally boards were counted every night, and the count went in a book, and the book went to the Flood Office. If forty-seven turned up in a ledger, it was no boat, Iona. It was a person someone needed to account for."

She closed her fingers around the brass. It was cold, then warm. Outside, the tide began to turn, and she decided she would find the lock's last count before the water came back.
