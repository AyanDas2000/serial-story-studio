# The Tide Ledger, episode 4

Approved prose from the existing author run.

Iona came back to Gate Nine at the bottom of the ebb with nothing in her hands. She had spent half the night in Bay Nine and found only a gap on the shelf where the levelling book for the east line should have stood, and a dust-free rectangle that told her someone had taken it before her.

"No book," Neri said, reading her face from across the stone.

"No book. I'm sorry."

Neri shrugged, but her mouth tightened. She had already set up the staff against the gate's footing, and the morning smelled of wet rope and diesel from the contractors' barge moored down the quay. "Then we measure what's here."

They worked without talking much. Neri called figures, and Iona wrote them in the margin of a shelf label because it was the only paper she had. The tide had not yet turned, but the culvert beneath the gate had begun to mutter. Neri knelt at the false line, the sharper one, and ran a thumbnail along the dressed edge. "This isn't mortar," she said. "It's lime putty, tinted. Someone patched here, and not long ago."

The bell cut across her words.

It came from the harbour office at the head of the quay: three strokes, short and flat, with a pause between each, the way a man rings when he wants a thing to be understood and not admired. Iona's pencil stopped. For one cold second she heard the number, forty-seven, and then she heard the plain difference. A number was something stamped on brass and hidden in a page. This was a signal, and it was meant to be obeyed.

"Tide gauge," Neri said, already rising. "Three short means the culvert's about to run. They clear the foundation cut before it floods." She snatched up the staff. "We've ten minutes."

"It's a bell, not a count," Iona said, half to herself.

"What?"

"Nothing. Go."

But Neri had not gone. She had dropped to her knees again at the patched stone, and with the iron shoe of the staff she levered at the tinted putty. It came away in a grey crust. Behind it, in a seam no wider than a finger, lay something pale and folded small.

"Leave it," Iona said, and then, because she was already kneeling, "No. Get it."

Neri worked it out with two fingers, careful as a surgeon. Water was beginning to slide over the lowest step. What came free was a single leaf, torn rather than cut, stiff with silt and folded in thirds. Down one edge ran a row of tiny holes, and through three of them hung a length of blue thread, thin and tightly twisted, still bright where the fold had kept it from the salt.

Iona knew that thread. She had threaded a needle with it every spring for eleven years, mending the spines of the oldest registers. The Flood Office bought it by the reel from a binder in the Lanterns, and no other office she knew of used the colour. She did not say so. She turned the leaf over with the edge of her glove. Columns again, ruled in faded ink: dates from the winter of the Surge, ward names, a heading half eaten by damp. The numbers beneath it were figures of some sort, not names, and she could not read them with the water hissing at her knees.

"That's a ledger page," Neri said. She had gone very still. "Isn't it? That's from a book."

"It's from a bound volume." Iona folded the leaf along its old creases and felt the thread catch against her glove. "Someone tore it from the gutter and put it where they thought the stone would keep it."

"Or hide it."

"Or both."

Down the quay the bell began again, three short, and men's voices followed it, boots on the planking. A foreman in an oilskin was walking toward them with the unhurried tread of a person who has always been obeyed. Neri snatched up her field book and staff. The patched seam gaped open behind her, grey and plain.

"They'll see where it was," she whispered.

"Yes."

"Then they'll know someone's been looking."

Iona looked at the leaf, then at Neri's black sleeve and frightened, stubborn face. She could carry the page up to the Flood Office, where it belonged, and watch the lower stacks be boxed around it. Or she could keep it against her ribs and become, from this moment, someone who had taken what was meant to stay buried.

She slid it inside her coat, beside the token, and stood to meet the foreman with her badge held out in front of her like a shield.
